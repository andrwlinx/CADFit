\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

The product of mesh-to-CAD reconstruction is a program. A designer edits its sketches and features, and a CAD kernel turns it into a solid. Optimization-based reconstructors such as CADFit \citep{nehme2026cadfit} build that program by proposing extrusions and revolves fitted to slices of the target mesh, composing them with booleans, and keeping the composition with the highest volumetric IoU. In the implementation we audit, the score that steers the search is computed on meshes: candidate solids are tessellated, combined, and compared with the target. The program written out at the end is a separate artifact, and whether it builds and reproduces the scored geometry is not part of the search's number.

Search extensions add compositions, booleans and proposal types, and each is a new way for the reported mesh and the final program to diverge. We therefore measure the divergence arm by arm instead of assuming it is constant. The same question applies to LLM agents for CAD \citep{mallis2025cadassistant,savrasov2026cadfather}, which give a language model geometric tools. CADFather \citep{savrasov2026cadfather} ablates its tools and leaves a controlled evaluation of the assistant's own choices among them to future work. Separating the two requires a non-LLM policy over the same tools and budget, scored on executed programs.

We study three questions on \nFresh{} hard-tier CADBench parts \citep{doris2026cadbench} drawn after, and disjoint from, every part used during development:
\begin{itemize}
\item[\textbf{Q1}] Does mesh IoU track the IoU of the executed program (\iouc) across search arms?
\item[\textbf{Q2}] Which search extensions improve the executed program, not just the reported mesh?
\item[\textbf{Q3}] Do the optimizer's own stages make open-weight LLMs competent CAD agents, and does the LLM add anything over a script that uses the same stages?
\end{itemize}

Our answers, summarised in \cref{fig:hero}:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Execution changes the ranking of search arms} (\cref{sec:search}). The mean gap between mesh IoU and \iouc{} is \armBaseGap{} for the base pipeline, \armBeamGap{} with beam search and \armSecGap{} with section proposals, and almost all of it comes from programs that do not build. Gains in \iouc{} are significantly smaller than gains in mesh IoU for the rule package and for the full ladder. We also document defects in the search objective and in our own extensions, including an alpha-wrapped objective, cavity filling and self-intersecting lofts, that change individual scores (\cref{sec:measure}).
\item \textbf{Whole-part section proposals survive execution} (\cref{sec:search}). Adding a mid-plane section extrusion per axis to the candidate pool is the largest observed \iouc{} increment among our extensions (\dSecBeamCadDiff, 95\% interval \dSecBeamCadCI). Over the base pipeline, the overall \iouc{} gain of all extensions together is not established, and it is concentrated on benchE.
\item \textbf{Optimizer stages are effective tools for open-weight agents} (\cref{sec:agents}). With the stages as tools, all six models improve over writing CadQuery alone, by \skillsGainMin{} to \skillsGainMax{} \iouc, and every Bonferroni-corrected interval excludes zero. The best agent scores \mGemmaSkills{} against \agentPipeSec{} for the strongest pipeline arm on the same parts (difference \mGemmaVsPipeDiff, interval \mGemmaVsPipeCI). Against a scripted policy with the same tools and call budget, \policyVerdict.
\end{enumerate}
